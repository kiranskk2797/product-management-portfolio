## 1. Executive Summary

This case study investigates why Zepto customers place small, urgency-driven orders and explores how product changes could increase Average Order Value (AOV) without compromising the platform's promise of rapid delivery.

The original assignment reports 45 survey responses, 10 user interviews and secondary research. However, the underlying respondent-level survey data and detailed interview notes or transcripts are not currently available for independent verification.

The central hypothesis is that ultra-fast delivery can encourage customers to split purchases across multiple orders instead of consolidating them into a single basket.

**Recommendation:** Prioritize a One-Tap Pack Size Upgrade that offers a relevant larger pack directly within the cart or checkout journey.

The solution is a product recommendation, not a deployed or experimentally validated feature.

## 2. Problem Statement

### Business problem

Zepto's quick-commerce experience optimizes for speed and convenience. However, the same convenience may reduce customers' motivation to consolidate purchases, resulting in smaller baskets and potentially less efficient fulfillment.

The original assignment identifies three reported behaviors:

- **Mission-based shopping:** Customers open the app intending to buy a specific item.
- **Threshold ceiling:** Customers stop adding products after reaching the free-delivery threshold.
- **Micro-order behavior:** Customers place multiple small orders because they can quickly order forgotten items later.

### Core problem statement

How might Zepto increase basket value and encourage appropriate purchase consolidation without adding friction to the fast-shopping experience?

This framing is a hypothesis derived from the original assignment, not a verified conclusion about Zepto's overall customer base.

## 4. Key Research Findings

The percentages below are reported in the original assignment. The underlying respondent-level data is unavailable for independent verification.

### Finding 1: Mission-based shopping

**62.2%** of survey respondents were reported to open the app with a specific shopping mission.

**Interpretation:** Customers prioritizing speed may be less likely to explore additional categories.

**Product implication:** Offer relevant upgrades within the existing purchase flow rather than requiring extra browsing.

### Finding 2: The free-delivery threshold ceiling

**77.8%** of respondents were reported to stop adding items after reaching the free-delivery threshold.

**Interpretation:** The threshold may act as a stopping point instead of encouraging continued shopping.

**Product implication:** Explore incentives that remain relevant after the initial threshold is reached.

### Finding 3: The micro-order safety net

**71.1%** of respondents were reported to place multiple small orders because they could quickly purchase forgotten items later.

**Interpretation:** The underlying problem may be one intended basket split across multiple orders, rather than simply a lack of products in each basket.

**Product implication:** Test whether appropriate pack-size upgrades can increase basket value without disrupting the user's immediate shopping mission.

These percentages are reported in the original assignment. The survey questionnaire, respondent-level data and detailed interview evidence should be retained or recollected to support further analysis.

## 6. Proposed Product Solutions

### Solution A: One-Tap Pack Size Upgrade

Offer a relevant larger pack when a customer adds a smaller pack.

**Example:** When a customer adds 500 ml of milk, offer a 1-litre pack as an alternative.

**Expected benefits:**
- Increase average item value.
- Reduce the need for additional browsing.
- Preserve the existing checkout journey.

**Primary risk:** A larger pack may not fit the customer's immediate need, household size, budget or consumption pattern.

The upgrade should be optional, contextually relevant and easy to dismiss.

### Solution B: Multi-Tier Milestone Rewards

Introduce additional incentives beyond the existing free-delivery threshold.

The original assignment proposed examples such as a reward around ₹349 and cashback around ₹499. These are illustrative proposal values from the assignment, not verified current Zepto thresholds, offers or policies.

**Expected benefit:** Encourage customers who respond to incentives to continue building their baskets.

**Risks:**
- Incentive costs and margin dilution.
- Encouraging unnecessary purchases.
- Customers changing purchase timing to obtain rewards.
- Rewards increasing order value without improving profitability.

Any implementation would require current policy checks, cost estimates and validation.

### Solution C: Contextual Mission Kits

Offer curated bundles such as a late-night study kit based on shopping context.

**Expected benefit:** Simplify discovery and encourage relevant cross-category purchases.

**Risks:**
- Poorly matched bundles may create decision fatigue.
- Recommendations may be irrelevant to the customer's immediate need.
- Bundle adoption and incremental contribution margin are unknown.

These three solutions are proposals from the original assignment, not implemented features.

## 7. Prioritization — RICE Framework

The original assignment scores the three proposed solutions as follows.

| Solution | Reach | Impact | Confidence | Effort | Original RICE score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| One-Tap Pack Size Upgrade | 9 | 9 | 8 | 4 | 162 |
| Milestone Rewards | 8 | 8 | 7 | 6 | 74.7 |
| Contextual Mission Kits | 5 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 26.3 |

**Original formula:** RICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort

### Methodology note

The scores shown above are the original assignment estimates. The accompanying 1–10 rubric is a proposed custom prioritization scale, not a validated or directly comparable implementation of conventional RICE. Reach, impact, confidence and effort have not been independently measured.

The scores are retained for transparency and should be recalibrated using consistent definitions, product data and delivery-team estimates before an implementation decision.

### RICE Scoring Rubric

These scores are preliminary estimates, not measured business outcomes.

| Dimension | Proposed scoring approach |
|---|---|
| Reach (1–10) | Relative estimate of target customers exposed to the solution during a defined period. |
| Impact (1–10) | Expected effect on AOV and the target customer behavior. |
| Confidence (1–10) | Confidence in the reach and impact estimates, based on available evidence. |
| Effort (1–10) | Relative implementation effort; higher scores indicate greater effort. |

### Rationale for the Original Scores

| Solution | Initial rationale | Key uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| One-Tap Pack Size Upgrade | Fits into the existing cart journey and aims to increase item value without additional browsing. | Eligible reach, upgrade acceptance, AOV impact and engineering effort are unknown. |
| Milestone Rewards | Aims to encourage additional spending among customers who respond to incentives. | Incremental spending, reward costs and contribution margin impact are unknown. |
| Contextual Mission Kits | Offers relevant bundles that may encourage cross-category purchases. | Customer demand, bundle adoption and implementation effort are unvalidated. |

These explanations describe product hypotheses; they do not validate the numerical inputs.

### Why Pack Size Upgrade Was Selected

- It targets customers during an existing purchase journey.
- It requires less additional discovery effort from the customer.
- It is designed to fit into the existing cart or checkout experience.
- Its estimated implementation effort is lower than the alternatives.

The selection is a directional recommendation based on the original estimates, not proof that the solution will produce the greatest business impact.

### Proposed Eligibility Rules

The upgrade should appear only when it is likely to offer meaningful value to the customer.

**Show the upgrade when:**
- A relevant larger pack of the same product is available.
- The larger pack offers a clear unit-price advantage or another understandable benefit.
- The customer can see the total price before accepting.
- The recommendation does not require leaving the current shopping flow.

**Do not show the upgrade when:**
- The larger pack is unavailable.
- The customer has explicitly dismissed the same recommendation.
- The larger quantity is unlikely to suit the shopping context.
- The upgrade introduces unnecessary friction into checkout.

**Customer control:** The upgrade remains optional. The original selection must be easy to retain, and the customer must be able to dismiss the recommendation.

**Validation needed:** Test whether these rules improve upgrade acceptance and AOV without harming checkout completion, contribution margin or customer satisfaction.

These are proposed product requirements, not implemented functionality.

### Prioritization Limitations

The RICE inputs are estimates from the original assignment, not measured values. Before making an implementation decision:

- Estimate reach using product data and defined eligibility criteria.
- Support impact estimates with research or experiment results.
- Document the evidence behind each confidence score.
- Validate effort estimates with the engineering and design teams.
- Apply consistent scoring definitions to all three solutions.

The current ranking should be treated as a directional hypothesis, not proof that the highest-scoring solution will produce the greatest business impact.

## 8. Proposed Experiment

The following experiment is a proposed next step, not an experiment conducted during the assignment.

### Hypothesis

Offering a relevant, optional larger pack at the cart or checkout stage will increase AOV without materially harming conversion or customer experience.

### Experiment Design

Compare two eligible customer groups:

- **Control:** Existing shopping and checkout experience.
- **Treatment:** Existing experience with an optional One-Tap Pack Size Upgrade.

Randomize eligible users and run the experiment for a period determined by the required sample size and normal purchase cycles.

The experiment would need a defined eligibility rule, a reliable assignment method and appropriate event tracking before launch.

### Metrics

The following are proposed metrics for a future experiment; no experiment has been run and no results are available.

**Primary metric**
- **Average Order Value (AOV):** Average completed order value per order.

**Supporting metrics**
- Average item value.
- Items per order.
- Pack-upgrade acceptance rate.
- Contribution margin per order, if reliable cost and margin data are available.

**Guardrail metrics**
- Checkout completion rate.
- Cart abandonment rate.
- Checkout time.
- Customer satisfaction and upgrade-related complaints, if these can be measured reliably.

The metrics listed here are a measurement plan, not data currently available to this case study. The team would need access to appropriate product analytics, cost data and customer-feedback signals before evaluating the experiment.

### Decision Criteria

Proceed only if the experiment demonstrates a meaningful improvement in AOV or contribution margin while meeting predefined guardrail limits.

The minimum detectable effect, required sample size, experiment duration and decision thresholds should be established before launch using baseline data and business requirements.

No numerical success threshold is proposed here because the necessary baseline data is unavailable.

## 9. Risks and Trade-offs

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Larger packs do not fit the user's needs | Show relevant, optional alternatives |
| Higher AOV comes with lower margins | Measure contribution margin, not AOV alone |
| Upsell prompts slow checkout | Track checkout time and completion |
| Discounts increase costs | Evaluate incremental margin after incentives |
| Findings do not generalize | Test across relevant customer segments |
| Larger packs create waste or over-purchasing | Make benefits transparent and keep the upgrade optional |
| Recommendations are irrelevant | Use product availability and relevance rules |

A higher AOV does not automatically mean higher profitability. Incremental revenue, product margins, incentive costs and fulfillment economics must be evaluated together.

## 11. Key Learnings

- Convenience can create unintended behavioral trade-offs.
- A product intervention should fit the user's existing workflow.
- A strong recommendation requires evidence that supports both the problem and the proposed mechanism.
- Prioritization frameworks are only as reliable as their scoring assumptions.
- AOV should be evaluated alongside contribution margin and customer-experience guardrails.
- Research limitations should be documented rather than hidden.
- A proposed experiment should define its metrics and decision criteria before launch.
